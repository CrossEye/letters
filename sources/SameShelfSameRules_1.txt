Sam Prentice writes that he has tried to understand what Democrats stand for. John Barnowski can speak for himself, but I’m glad to help with what Ellen Paul and I actually said.

Mr. Prentice says that using school libraries to get around parents is our most important issue. I’d invite him to reread my letters. This fall I have written about the $630,000 in new state aid for our five towns that Rep. Steve Weir voted against, about pool fences, about a tenant-safety bill that died on the session’s last night, about nursing-home transparency, and about wage theft. Even my Banned Books Week letter turned halfway through to hospital investors and absentee landlords.

He calls it outrageous that Ellen’s law “protects a library’s ability to carry books like the Bible as much as it protects books about gender identity.” That sentence doesn’t say the two books are morally equal. It says the same rules apply to both, which is the only way rules protect anyone. The law bars removing a book because of the origin or views of its author. That guards his shelf against people who dislike his views exactly as it guards other shelves against him.

This is not hypothetical. In 2023 a Utah school district, acting on one person’s challenge under a state law aimed at sexual content, pulled the Bible from its elementary and junior high libraries for vulgarity and violence. Anyone who has read Genesis 19, Judges 19, or 2 Samuel 11 knows what a review committee would find there. I don’t think those chapters make the Bible unfit for a library. I think they show why no single objector should decide what is on the shelf for everyone else’s child.

He compares reviewing books to movie ratings. But ratings don’t pull an R-rated film from every theater because one patron objects. They inform parents, and each family decides. Connecticut’s law works on the same principle: every library needs a written policy for what it collects, and anyone who objects to a title gets a fair, consistent process.

Nor is there any contradiction in raising the marriage age. A marriage binds a child to a legal contract with an adult. A library book binds no one to anything, and a parent can march it back to the desk. That law set the minimum age for marriage at 18; it had nothing to do with the age of consent. The Senate passed it unanimously, the House 98 to 45, and Connecticut became the ninth state to end child marriage. Rep. Weir voted no.

He asks why Democrats care about children who are not their own. Caring about other people’s children is the whole premise of a public school, a public library, and a pool-fence law. That bill passed the House 102 to 45; Weir voted no. As for starting education at six months, I’m not sure which proposal he means. The 2025 early childhood care and education bill passed 133 to 10, with 35 Republicans voting yes. Weir was one of the ten. If it was a plot to control our children, most House Republicans were in on it.

On the minimum wage, Mr. Prentice repeats a theory I have answered here since 2016 with the research: Card and Krueger in 1994, Dube, Lester and Reich in 2010, and the work since, which finds that increases raise pay for the lowest-paid with little effect on jobs or prices. He has never answered with evidence. It matters locally, too: Rep. Weir has twice put his name on bills to cut the minimum wage for workers under 18. To his credit, he voted for this year’s workforce-development bill, exactly the kind of skills-building Mr. Prentice favors.

He ends by saying we need more children and fewer Democrats. I’d settle for more children in well-funded schools, choosing from shelves their parents and librarians, not the loudest objector, helped fill. On Nov. 3, that means Ellen Paul.
